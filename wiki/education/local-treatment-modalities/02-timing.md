# Put the disease setting before the clock

**You will learn to describe the job of a local treatment before discussing its timing.** Start with [the mechanism lesson](/wiki/education/local-treatment-modalities/01-mechanism) and [neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/neoadjuvant-vs-adjuvant) if treatment-setting language is new.

## The intuition: a date is meaningful only within a plan

“Before or after immunotherapy?” sounds like one question. It can hide several different ones. Are we trying to relieve pain, prevent growth at a vulnerable site, lower local recurrence risk after surgery, or test whether local injury adds to a systemic response? Each goal has its own comparison, constraints and evidence.

[Ablation](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/ablation) and external [radiation therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/radiation-therapy-basics) act at selected sites. They can have a useful local job even when an added immune effect is uncertain. Conversely, successful treatment of selected sites does not establish control of cancer elsewhere.

## Name the disease setting

These terms describe patterns; each study supplies its own eligibility definition:

| Setting | What the label describes | What it leaves unanswered |
|---|---|---|
| Nonmetastatic disease | Cancer without established distant metastases | Which local and systemic components improve outcomes in this stage and subtype |
| Oligometastatic disease | A limited number or extent of detected metastases | Whether treating every detected site adds benefit to systemic treatment |
| Oligoprogressive disease | A limited set of sites grows while other disease remains controlled | Whether treating the growing sites improves outcomes or safely extends a systemic strategy |
| Oligo-residual disease | A limited amount of visible disease remains after a broader treatment response | Whether consolidating those sites adds benefit in that specific setting |

The words are not interchangeable. A trial enrolling people after a response has already selected a different population from a trial enrolling people whose tumors are growing despite treatment.

A blood signal also differs from a mapped lesion. [Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/cfdna-vs-ctdna) is tumor-derived DNA measured in a sample, where DNA means deoxyribonucleic acid. A positive result alone does not provide a location, boundary or safe path for a probe or radiation plan. Earlier detection is another evidence question; see [lead-time bias](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/reasoning-tools/lead-time-bias).

## Worked example: three timing questions

**Fictional examples.** Maya has nonmetastatic breast cancer and is learning why radiation may follow surgery. Her question concerns local recurrence prevention within an established treatment sequence. Ravi has a painful bone metastasis. His question concerns symptom relief and protection of nearby structures. Jo has several metastases controlled by a systemic drug but one growing lesion. Her question concerns oligoprogression and the value of adding site-directed treatment.

All three might hear the word “radiation.” Their studies, goals and clocks are different. An immune signal measured in Jo's study would not establish the benefit of Maya's postoperative treatment. Ravi's pain improvement would be meaningful even without shrinkage of untreated tumors.

Notice what we have not chosen: a dose, modality or date. First we have identified what a timing decision needs to accomplish.

## How timing and fractionation interact with the goal

External radiation is often divided into fractions, or treatment sessions. [Hypofractionation](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/hypofractionation) uses larger doses per fraction than a specified conventional comparator. Fewer sessions do not automatically make a course [stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/sbrt). Treatment volume, planning, normal-tissue constraints and the clinical evidence all matter.

FAST-Forward studied postoperative whole-breast or chest-wall radiation in a defined early-breast-cancer population. It is evidence about local control and normal-tissue effects for the tested schedules. It did not test an optimal immune-priming schedule or turn every short breast-radiation course into SBRT. [Brunt and colleagues, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32580883/).

Other clocks include recovery, healing, prior exposure, overlapping toxicity and protocol assessment times. Their presence is a reason to ask how a sequence was studied, not to invent a universal interval between treatments.

## Common failure modes

Transferring a sequence across cancers skips the population question. Choosing a date from a laboratory immune signal skips clinical outcomes and safety. Repeating a local procedure whenever a blood marker rises skips anatomy, cumulative injury and evidence that the proposed response improves care.

Keep the timing question in a sentence: “In this disease setting, does adding this treatment at this point improve this outcome compared with the alternative?” That sentence is much more informative than “earlier must be better.”

## Try it

A fictional proposal cites a lung-cancer trial in people with a few residual lesions after an immunotherapy response. It proposes immediate ablation for a person with a positive blood test but no mapped lesion. What changed between the study and the proposal?

**Answer:** The cancer population, disease setting, anatomical target and tested action changed. A blood finding alone does not establish a treatable site. The cited sequence cannot supply the missing evidence.

## Explain it back

Explain why “only a few lesions” does not tell you whether disease is oligometastatic, oligoprogressive or oligo-residual. Include what happened before the current scan.

## Takeaway

Start with the goal and disease history. Timing belongs to a tested treatment strategy, rather than to a general theory that local injury should happen as early as possible.
