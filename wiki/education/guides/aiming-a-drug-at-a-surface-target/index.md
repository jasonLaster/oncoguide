# Aiming a drug at a surface target

**Explain how antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, engagers and radioligands find and kill tumor cells, which breast-cancer targets and payloads matter, and what evidence a claim still needs.**

When a report names a protein on a tumor cell, the next question is often, “Can we aim a treatment at it?” A promising address is a useful beginning, but it can support several product ideas, and an antibody, a chemical payload, a recruited T cell, a radionuclide or an engineered receptor each brings a different action and a different set of requirements. This guide follows that question through recognition, delivery, evidence, payload choice and target choice. The anchor examples are the antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) used in breast cancer: sacituzumab govitecan, trastuzumab deruxtecan and datopotamab deruxtecan.

The core path takes about 86 minutes. Start with [antibody structure](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/antibody-structure) if the distinction between binding and other antibody functions is new. [Chemotherapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/chemotherapy) introduces chemical damage before you meet antibody-linked payloads.

## The big ideas

1. A surface protein can be an address without being a process the tumor depends on.
2. Binding, delivery, processing, activation, and killing are separate measurements.
3. The recognition route determines whether a particular peptide displayed by [human leukocyte antigen (HLA)](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/hla) is required.
4. The same target can create different risks when paired with different therapeutic actions and exposures.
5. Clinical benefit belongs to a defined product, population, setting, and comparison.
6. When a targeted drug stops working, find which part failed before changing the target; a new address does not fix a payload problem.
7. Every new payload strategy is a biological bet about how tumor cells survive; judge it by the failure it addresses and the evidence that would settle it.
8. A target earns its place on four separate counts: how much of it there is, where else it appears, whether the chosen format's mechanism works on it, and how evenly it is spread.

## Build an evidence ledger

Carry one target through the whole path. For every proposed product, write the observation, the conclusion it supports, the next unresolved question, and the measurement that could answer it.

For example, a tissue stain can support a protein signal. Live-cell testing can add target-associated binding. A delivery assay can add processing information. A functional assay can add activity in its model. A clinical comparison asks about outcomes in people. The ledger helps you preserve useful evidence while keeping each inference within its limits.

If your starting point is a gene alteration rather than a surface measurement, begin with [From a variant to a drug claim](/wiki/education/guides/from-variant-to-drug/index). For deeper cell behavior, continue to [engineered cell therapy](/wiki/education/guides/engineered-cell-therapy/index). [Experimental therapy access](/wiki/education/guides/accessing-experimental-therapy/index) covers practical access gates after the evidence question is clear.

## What is still uncertain

A target can be unevenly distributed or inaccessible in a living tumor. A model can omit immune cells, normal tissues, or realistic exposure. A product can produce measurable activity while its clinical benefit remains unknown in the intended setting. Each uncertainty belongs beside the claim it limits.
