# Clinical trials versus expanded access

## In one sentence

A clinical trial studies a treatment under a research protocol, while expanded access uses an investigational product for treatment outside a trial under applicable safeguards.

## The intuition

A trial is a planned way to learn whether a treatment helps and how it harms. Expanded access considers treatment for someone who cannot use a suitable trial route. Both involve uncertainty, but their primary purposes differ.

Think of two pathways with different questions at the entrance. The analogy stops at access: neither pathway is a reservation for a dose. The clinician, supplier and review bodies each have a part to play.

## How it works

A **clinical trial** follows a research protocol specifying eligibility, treatment, monitoring and outcomes. Participants may be [randomized](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/trial-phases-and-randomization); joining does not always mean receiving the investigational product.

**Expanded access**, also called compassionate use, permits treatment use outside a trial in qualifying circumstances. U.S. drug and biologic criteria include serious or immediately life-threatening illness, no comparable or satisfactory alternative, potential benefit that justifies the risk, and no interference with clinical development. For an individual patient, additional determinations concern the risk from treatment versus disease and inability to obtain the drug under another investigational application or protocol. A closed trial cohort alone does not settle these criteria.

A single-patient [Investigational New Drug (IND) application](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/ind-vs-bla) is a **new application**. An individual-patient **expanded-access protocol** is submitted under an existing active IND. The existing sponsor generally submits that protocol. A physician's separate IND may instead refer to the sponsor's supporting information with permission. Having the same drug does not make these submissions interchangeable.

The supplier must agree to provide product and appropriate supporting information. A licensed physician needs a treatment and monitoring plan. The **Food and Drug Administration (FDA)** evaluates the applicable request. **Institutional review board (IRB)** review and informed consent requirements also apply, with specific emergency exceptions and individual-patient review procedures.

| Question | Clinical trial | Expanded access |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Answer research questions | Treat qualifying patients outside a trial |
| Entry | Protocol eligibility and available site/cohort | Access criteria, supply and applicable reviews |
| Assignment | Protocol-defined, sometimes randomized | Defined access treatment plan |
| Evidence | Strength depends on study design and conduct | Does not replace controlled efficacy evidence |

### Name the clock and its starting event

The April 2026 FDA guidance distinguishes these routes. The time rules below concern U.S. drugs and biologics, not the full preparation schedule.

| Submission or circumstance | Rule and remaining gates |
|---|---|
| New individual-patient IND, non-emergency | Effective **30 calendar days after FDA receipt**, unless held, or on earlier FDA notification that use may begin. Applicable IRB review and consent must be completed. |
| Individual-patient protocol under an existing active IND, non-emergency | No automatic 30-day waiting period. The protocol must be submitted to FDA and obtain applicable IRB approval before use; FDA can impose a clinical hold. Consent still applies. |
| Emergency individual-patient IND or protocol | Prior FDA authorization may be given by telephone or other rapid communication. The written follow-up is due within **15 working days of FDA authorization**. |
| Emergency use without time for prospective IRB review | Notify the IRB within **5 working days of emergency use**. This exception is specific to that circumstance. |

The no-wait protocol rule is not universal: a widespread-use **treatment protocol** under an existing IND has a 30-day rule or earlier FDA notification. Emergency procedures address an acute need for treatment before a written submission can be made; a time-sensitive chronic-treatment plan is not automatically an emergency.

Supplier review, manufacture, shipping, institutional readiness and clinical scheduling have separate dependencies. Calendar days and working days cannot be silently swapped. No row promises a treatment start date.

## Why it matters in cancer

Permission to proceed answers an access question. It does not establish benefit in a disease setting, assure usable supply or create a team that can manage toxicity. [Federal Right to Try](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/right-to-try) is another pathway with distinct eligibility and review rules.

## Common confusions

- Compassionate use does not mean the treatment is known to work.
- Supplier agreement does not replace clinician responsibility or applicable review.
- A platform's previous authorization does not cover every personalized formulation.
- An individual-patient protocol is not automatically a new individual-patient IND.

## Try it

Two fictional non-emergency requests are prepared. A physician submits a new individual IND; an existing sponsor submits an individual protocol under its active IND. Does each require a 30-calendar-day wait?

**Answer:** No. The new IND has that rule or earlier FDA notification, absent a hold. The individual protocol has no automatic 30-day wait once submitted and appropriately reviewed. Neither route establishes complete treatment readiness or a promised date.
