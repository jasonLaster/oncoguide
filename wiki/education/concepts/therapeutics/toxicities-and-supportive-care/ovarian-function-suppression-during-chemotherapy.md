# Ovarian function suppression during chemotherapy

## In one sentence

Ovarian function suppression during chemotherapy uses a gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist to quiet the ovaries while chemotherapy is given, lowering the chance that treatment causes early menopause.

## The intuition

Think of the ovaries as a workshop that chemotherapy can damage while it is busy. A GnRH agonist hangs a "closed for now" sign on the door: the hormonal signal that drives the ovaries stops, and the workshop goes quiet until the drug is stopped. Fewer people find the workshop permanently shut afterward. The analogy breaks in one place: nobody is sure that "quiet" is the whole reason it works, and a quiet workshop is not a guarantee that eggs survive in the numbers needed for a later pregnancy.

## How it works

The brain's hypothalamus releases GnRH in pulses. Those pulses tell the pituitary gland to release the two hormones that drive the ovaries, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone. A **GnRH agonist**, such as goserelin or triptorelin, is given by injection every four weeks. After a brief surge, constant stimulation makes the pituitary stop responding, so FSH and luteinizing hormone fall and the ovaries become inactive. Periods usually stop during treatment and return after the drug is stopped, if the ovaries still work.

[Chemotherapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/chemotherapy) can destroy the pool of immature eggs. The risk is highest with [alkylating agents](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/alkylating-agents) such as cyclophosphamide, and it rises with age at treatment. Loss of that pool causes **premature ovarian insufficiency**: periods stop, FSH rises into the postmenopausal range, and natural fertility ends early.

In PROMISE-GIM6, the agonist was started at least a week before the first chemotherapy dose and continued every four weeks until chemotherapy ended [3]. Why this protects the ovaries is still debated. Proposed explanations include fewer follicles being recruited into the vulnerable growing stage and changes in blood flow to the ovary. The clinical effect is better established than the mechanism.

## The evidence in three studies

| Study | Who was enrolled | Main result |
|---|---|---|
| POEMS/SWOG S0230 [1, 2] | 257 premenopausal women with operable hormone-receptor-negative breast cancer, randomized to chemotherapy with or without goserelin | Ovarian failure at 2 years: 8% with goserelin versus 22% without (odds ratio 0.30), among 135 women with complete data. Five-year cumulative pregnancy rate 23.1% versus 12.2% |
| PROMISE-GIM6 [3] | 281 premenopausal women with stage I–III breast cancer, randomized to chemotherapy with or without triptorelin | Early menopause one year after chemotherapy: 8.9% versus 25.9% (odds ratio 0.28) |
| Individual-patient meta-analysis [4] | 873 patients from five randomized breast cancer trials | Premature ovarian insufficiency 14.1% versus 30.9% (adjusted odds ratio 0.38). At least one later pregnancy in 10.3% versus 5.5%. No difference in disease-free survival (adjusted hazard ratio 1.01) |

POEMS matters especially for triple-negative disease because it enrolled only hormone-receptor-negative cancers. Its survival comparisons were secondary and not statistically significant (disease-free survival hazard ratio 0.55, P = .09) [2]. They show that the agonist did not appear to harm cancer outcomes, not that it improves them. See [odds ratio](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/effect-measures/odds-ratio) and [meta-analysis](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/meta-analysis) for how to read these measures.

## Why it matters in cancer

- Many people with triple-negative breast cancer are premenopausal when they start chemotherapy, and the standard early-stage regimens include cyclophosphamide.
- Early menopause has effects beyond fertility, including hot flashes, bone loss and changes in heart and sexual health. Lowering its risk is a supportive-care goal in its own right.
- The 2025 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guideline says a GnRH agonist **may be offered as an adjunct** to women with breast cancer, but it **should not be used in place of** established fertility preservation methods [5].

## Common confusions

- **Ovarian protection is not fertility preservation.** Freezing eggs, embryos or ovarian tissue before chemotherapy are the established ways to preserve fertility [5]. A GnRH agonist lowers the risk of ovarian failure; it does not store eggs, and the trials were not designed to prove it preserves fertility on its own. The two can be combined, and any freezing has to be arranged before chemotherapy starts.
- **This is not hormone therapy for the cancer.** In hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer, ovarian suppression is also used for years after chemotherapy to lower estrogen and reduce recurrence. That is a different job, with different trials. In triple-negative disease the cancer does not depend on estrogen, so the only aim here is to protect the ovaries.
- **Periods returning does not mean fertility is normal.** Ovarian reserve can be reduced even when cycles resume.
- **No periods during treatment is expected.** It is the drug working, not proof of permanent menopause.

## Try it

A trial reports that early menopause fell from 22% to 8% with an agonist. A friend concludes that the agonist guarantees she can become pregnant later. What does the result support?

**Answer:** It supports a lower risk of ovarian failure in that trial's population. It does not guarantee any individual's fertility, and it was not a comparison with egg or embryo freezing. Pregnancy rates were higher with the agonist, but most women in both groups did not report a pregnancy.

## Explain it back

"A GnRH agonist during chemotherapy lowers ___, but it is not a substitute for ___."

**One answer:** the chance that chemotherapy causes early menopause; egg or embryo freezing when fertility preservation is wanted.

## Takeaway

**A GnRH agonist given during chemotherapy cuts the risk of chemotherapy-induced early menopause by about half or more in randomized breast cancer trials, without a detected effect on cancer outcomes; fertility preservation is a separate decision.**
