# Pulmonary function testing

## In one sentence

Pulmonary function testing measures how much air the lungs move, how much they hold and how well oxygen crosses into the blood, using spirometry, lung volumes and the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO).

## The intuition

Picture the lungs as a set of bellows feeding a very thin sheet where air meets blood. Spirometry asks how hard and how fast the bellows can push air out. A lung volume test asks how big the bellows are. DLCO asks how easily gas crosses the sheet. Inflammation or scarring of that sheet, as in drug-related lung injury, often shows up as a falling DLCO. The analogy breaks because the tests describe a pattern of function, not a cause: many different diseases produce the same numbers.

## How it works

Three measurements make up a full set of pulmonary function tests (PFTs) [4]:

| Test | What the person does | Main numbers | What a low value suggests |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spirometry** | Breathes in fully, then blows out as hard and as long as possible into a mouthpiece | **FEV1**, the forced expiratory volume in one second; **FVC**, the forced vital capacity (all the air blown out); and the FEV1/FVC ratio | A low ratio means **obstruction**: air cannot get out quickly, as in asthma or emphysema |
| **Lung volumes** | Breathes through a mouthpiece, often seated in a sealed booth (body plethysmography) | **TLC**, the total lung capacity | A low TLC confirms **restriction**: the lungs cannot fill fully, as in scarring or interstitial lung disease |
| **DLCO** | Breathes in a gas mixture with a trace of carbon monoxide and a tracer gas, holds the breath about 10 seconds, then breathes out | How much carbon monoxide crossed into the blood, in mL per minute per mmHg | Gas transfer is impaired: thickened or inflamed lung tissue, lost air sacs, or reduced blood flow through the lungs |

Each result is compared with values expected for a healthy person of the same age, sex and height. Current international standards classify a result as abnormal when it falls below the **lower limit of normal** for that reference population, rather than below a fixed percent of predicted [1]. DLCO also depends on the blood's hemoglobin level, so laboratories adjust the result for anemia, and people are asked not to smoke for several hours beforehand [2, 4].

## When they are used for drug-related lung disease

Several cancer treatments can inflame the lungs: checkpoint inhibitors, antibody-drug conjugates carrying deruxtecan, radiation and some chemotherapies; see [pneumonitis](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/pneumonitis). PFTs play three roles:

1. **Baseline.** A test before treatment records a person's own starting point. A later DLCO is far easier to read against her own earlier value than against a population average.
2. **Diagnosis, as one input.** An Italian multidisciplinary expert panel listed spirometry and DLCO among the core steps in working up suspected drug-induced interstitial lung disease, alongside history, oxygen saturation, laboratory tests to exclude infection, and computed tomography (CT) [3]. In interstitial disease, the usual pattern is a low DLCO, often with a restrictive pattern.
3. **Follow-up.** Repeating the test shows whether lung function is recovering or getting worse after the drug is stopped and treatment is given [4].

The drug labels set the limits of that role. The trastuzumab deruxtecan label tells clinicians to monitor for symptoms and evaluate suspected lung disease **by radiographic imaging**; it does not require scheduled PFTs [5]. CT and the clinical picture make the diagnosis. PFTs measure how much function is affected.

## Why it matters in cancer

- Lung injury from deruxtecan-carrying antibody-drug conjugates is a labeled, sometimes fatal risk: in DESTINY-Breast04, adjudicated drug-related interstitial lung disease occurred in 12.1% of patients given trastuzumab deruxtecan, with 0.8% fatal [6].
- A prior lung event, such as checkpoint pneumonitis, can affect eligibility for later drugs and trials. A measured baseline makes later comparisons possible.
- Radiation to the chest and some chemotherapies can each lower lung function, so a fall in DLCO has several possible causes in someone treated with more than one of them.

## Common confusions

- **A low DLCO is not a diagnosis of pneumonitis.** Anemia, emphysema, blood clots in the lungs and pulmonary hypertension also lower it [4].
- **Normal spirometry does not rule out interstitial lung disease.** Spirometry measures airflow; gas transfer can be impaired while airflow is normal.
- **Percent predicted is not the whole story.** Standards now prefer the lower limit of normal and attention to change over time within the same person [1].
- **PFTs do not replace CT.** Imaging shows where and what kind of change is present; PFTs show how much it costs in function.
- **Effort matters.** Spirometry depends on a maximal breath. A poor-effort test can look abnormal.

## Try it

A woman on an antibody-drug conjugate has a new dry cough. Her spirometry is normal, but DLCO has fallen from her baseline. Her hemoglobin has also dropped. What can you conclude?

**Answer:** Gas transfer may be impaired, but the falling hemoglobin alone can lower DLCO, so the value needs hemoglobin correction. Normal spirometry does not exclude interstitial lung disease. The cough and any fall in corrected DLCO call for prompt assessment with CT by the treating team; the PFT result alone cannot name the cause.

## Explain it back

"Spirometry measures ___, lung volumes measure ___, and DLCO measures ___; in drug-related lung disease, the test most likely to fall is ___."

**One answer:** how much and how fast air moves out; how much air the lungs hold; how well gas crosses into the blood; DLCO.

## Takeaway

**Pulmonary function tests measure airflow, lung size and gas transfer; in drug-related lung injury they set a baseline and track the cost to function, while symptoms and CT make the diagnosis.**
