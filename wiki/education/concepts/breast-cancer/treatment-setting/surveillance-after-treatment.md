# Surveillance after treatment for early breast cancer

## In one sentence

Surveillance after curative treatment for early breast cancer means regular visits and yearly mammography to look for returning or new cancer, with other scans, blood tests and tumor markers reserved for symptoms or findings.

## The intuition

A smoke alarm and a fire inspection do different jobs. Searching every room with a thermal camera each week would find a smoldering fire sooner. The real question is whether finding it sooner changes what happens. For breast cancer follow-up, randomized trials tested that question directly.

The analogy has a limit. Those trials are decades old, and both imaging and treatment for recurrent cancer have changed since.

## How it works

The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guideline, updated in 2006 and reaffirmed without changes in 2013, recommends [1]:

| Part of follow-up | ASCO recommendation for people without symptoms |
|---|---|
| History and physical examination | Every 3–6 months for the first 3 years, every 6–12 months in years 4 and 5, then yearly |
| Mammography | After breast-conserving surgery, the first follow-up mammogram 1 year after the mammogram that led to diagnosis and at least 6 months after radiation; then yearly |
| Routine blood counts, chemistry panels, bone scans, chest X-rays, liver or pelvic ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) | Not recommended |
| Tumor markers: carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen (CA) 15-3, CA 27.29 | Not recommended |

The American Cancer Society and ASCO survivorship guideline adds that data "do not support" routine laboratory or imaging tests without symptoms. It also covers late effects of treatment, screening for new cancers and healthy living [2]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guideline, as summarized in a 2026 National Cancer Institute training presentation, is similar. It lists visits one to four times a year for 5 years, then yearly, and a mammogram every 12 months. It does not call for routine imaging of a reconstructed breast, or for tests looking for metastases without symptoms [3]. People at high inherited risk, such as carriers of germline *BRCA1* or *BRCA2* variants, follow separate high-risk screening guidance, which can include breast MRI.

New or persistent symptoms between visits, such as bone pain, cough, headache or a new lump, are a reason to test whatever the schedule says.

## What the follow-up trials showed

Two Italian randomized trials in the 1980s and 1990s compared intensive testing with clinical follow-up:

- **GIVIO:** 1,320 women with stage I–III breast cancer. Intensive surveillance added bone scans, liver ultrasound, chest X-rays and blood tests. After a median 71 months, 20% had died with intensive follow-up and 18% with clinical follow-up. Quality of life did not differ [4].
- **Rosselli Del Turco:** 1,243 women. Chest X-rays and bone scans every 6 months found more chest and bone metastases earlier (112 versus 71). Five-year mortality was 18.6% versus 19.5% [5].

A Cochrane review pooling the two trials (2,563 women) found no overall-survival difference: hazard ratio 0.98 (95% confidence interval 0.84–1.15) [6]. Earlier detection moved the date of diagnosis without moving the date of death. Survival counted from the date of detection would therefore have made intensive testing look better, which is [lead-time bias](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/reasoning-tools/lead-time-bias); comparing deaths after randomization avoids it. The review notes the trials were run long ago. No comparable modern randomized trial has settled whether today's imaging plus today's treatment of recurrence would change the result.

## Blood tests for tumor DNA are still investigational

[Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/cfdna-vs-ctdna) tests look for tumor-derived DNA in blood, often with a [tumor-informed assay](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/tumor-informed-vs-tumor-naive-mrd-assays), to find [minimal residual disease](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/mrd) before scans show recurrence. A positive test is strongly linked to later relapse. That makes it prognostic, but it does not show that acting on it helps.

- **c-TRAK TN** tested ctDNA surveillance in early triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Of 161 people monitored, 27.3% had ctDNA detected by 12 months. Among those allocated to treatment, 72% (23 of 32) already had metastases on scans when ctDNA turned positive. None of the 5 who started pembrolizumab cleared ctDNA durably, and 7 people relapsed without a prior positive test [7].
- The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) concluded in 2022 that molecular residual disease detection "cannot be recommended in routine clinical practice," because there was no evidence yet that using it to direct treatment helps [8].

The [lead time](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/lead-time) a blood test provides and its [disagreements with imaging](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/ctdna-imaging-discordance) are separate questions from benefit.

## Why it matters in cancer

- In TNBC, recurrence risk is highest in the first 3–5 years, which is why visits are most frequent early; see [recurrence timing](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/natural-history/tnbc-recurrence-timing-and-sites).
- Follow-up also catches new breast cancers, late effects such as heart, nerve or bone problems, and needs such as genetic counseling.
- More tests are not free: false alarms, radiation exposure, anxiety and cost come with them.

## Common confusions

- **More testing is not better outcomes.** The randomized trials found earlier detection without longer survival.
- **A normal tumor marker does not rule out recurrence**, and a high one does not locate it.
- **Surveillance is not treatment.** A test schedule changes when cancer is found; whether that changes outcomes is a separate question.
- **The schedule is for people without symptoms.** New symptoms call for evaluation now.
- **A positive ctDNA result is a warning, not a diagnosis of where cancer is.** Scans and clinical assessment come next.

## Try it

A fictional friend finished treatment for early breast cancer a year ago and feels well. She asks why her team will not order a yearly CT scan and CA 15-3 test "just to be safe." What does the evidence say?

**Answer:** Randomized trials of intensive testing found earlier detection of metastases but no longer survival or better quality of life, and guidelines do not recommend routine scans or tumor markers without symptoms. Visits, yearly mammography and prompt testing for new symptoms are the evidence-based plan.

## Explain it back

"Routine scans after early breast cancer found recurrence ___, but did not ___."

**One possible answer:** "Earlier, in randomized trials reported in 1994; improve survival or quality of life, so guidelines reserve them for symptoms."

## Takeaway

**After curative treatment, follow-up is regular visits and yearly mammography; extra scans, tumor markers and ctDNA tests have not been shown to help people without symptoms live longer.**
