# Education concepts

Use a concept to answer one question; use a [guide](/wiki/education/index) to learn toward a goal. These summaries and the glossary definitions are generated from the concept pages. Domain and learner review remain pending where stated on each page.

## Access and regulation

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Companion diagnostics](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/companion-diagnostics) | A companion diagnostic supplies information essential to the safe and effective use of a corresponding treatment in its specified context. |
| [Drug approval pathways and expedited designations](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/fda-approval-types) | Drug approval authorizes a specified use, while expedited designations change parts of development or review without themselves authorizing marketing. |
| [Drug labeling and off-label use](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/drug-label-and-off-label-use) | Off-label use means a clinician uses an approved drug outside its approved labeling, while the evidence for that particular use must be assessed separately. |
| [Research-use, laboratory certification and test authorization](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/ruo-ldt-clia-and-fda-tiers) | Research-use labeling, laboratory certification and product authorization answer different questions about a test and do not form one universal ladder of clinical usefulness. |
| [Clinical trials versus expanded access](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/clinical-trial-vs-expanded-access) | A clinical trial studies a treatment under a research protocol, while expanded access uses an investigational product for treatment outside a trial under applicable safeguards. |
| [Finding and checking a clinical trial](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/finding-a-trial) | Finding a trial means identifying a study and then confirming its protocol, relevant cohort, site availability and formal eligibility with the study team. |
| [IND investigation versus BLA marketing review](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/ind-vs-bla) | An investigational application supports defined human use during research or access, while a marketing application asks for authorization to market a product for a specified use. |
| [Investigator-initiated trials](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/investigator-initiated-trials) | An investigator-initiated trial starts with a researcher's study proposal, while sponsor responsibilities, product oversight and participant protections still depend on the actual protocol and regulatory arrangement. |

## Breast cancer and pathology

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/dcis) | Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) describes abnormal breast cells confined to a milk duct, without invasion into the surrounding breast tissue. |
| [Histologic grade](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/histologic-grade) | Histologic grade describes a breast cancer's microscopic features; the Nottingham system combines tubule formation, nuclear appearance and mitotic activity into grades 1, 2 or 3. |
| [Ki-67 proliferation index](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/ki-67) | Ki-67 is a protein associated with the active cell cycle; its proliferation index is the percentage of evaluated tumor-cell nuclei that stain positive under a specified method. |
| [TNM staging](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/tnm-staging) | TNM staging describes the primary tumor, regional lymph nodes and distant metastasis using a cancer-specific classification. |
| [TNBC recurrence: timing and sites](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/natural-history/tnbc-recurrence-timing-and-sites) | Recurrence patterns describe when and where triple-negative breast cancer returns in a defined group after treatment. |
| [Estrogen receptor (ER)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/estrogen-receptor) | The estrogen receptor (ER) is a protein inside cells that responds to estrogen; in breast cancer, ER status describes tumor-cell receptor staining under validated testing rules. |
| [HER2: receptor biology and test status](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/her2) | Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is a cell-surface signaling protein; clinical HER2 status describes overexpression or gene amplification under breast-cancer testing rules. |
| [Intrinsic breast-cancer subtype](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/intrinsic-subtype) | An intrinsic breast-cancer subtype is a category assigned by comparing a tumor sample's gene-expression pattern with reference patterns. |
| [Progesterone receptor (PR)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/progesterone-receptor) | The progesterone receptor (PR) is a protein inside cells that responds to progesterone; breast-cancer PR status reports receptor staining in tumor-cell nuclei. |
| [Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/tnbc) | Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is breast cancer classified as negative for estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) by validated tissue tests. |
| [Pathologic complete response (pCR)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/pcr) | Pathologic complete response means no residual invasive cancer in the breast or sampled regional nodes after neoadjuvant treatment. |
| [Residual cancer burden (RCB)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/residual-cancer-burden) | Residual cancer burden combines breast tumor-bed measurements and regional-node findings into a score after neoadjuvant treatment. |
| [Surgical margins](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/surgical-margins) | A surgical margin is the edge of removed tissue, examined to determine whether cancer reaches that edge. |
| [Neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/neoadjuvant-vs-adjuvant) | Neoadjuvant and adjuvant describe treatment's timing around the main treatment, usually surgery: neoadjuvant comes before it, and adjuvant comes after it to lower recurrence risk. |

## Cancer biology

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Autophagy: recycling through lysosomes](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/autophagy) | Autophagy delivers cellular material to lysosomes for breakdown and reuse; measuring how much material is present does not establish how fast it moves. |
| [Ferroptosis: death through uncontrolled lipid oxidation](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/ferroptosis) | Ferroptosis is a form of cell death driven by iron-dependent damage to membrane lipids when protective systems cannot contain it. |
| [Immunogenic cell death](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/immunogenic-cell-death) | Immunogenic cell death is regulated cell death that can initiate an antigen-specific adaptive immune response in a compatible, immune-competent host. |
| [Ubiquitin and the proteasome](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/ubiquitin-and-proteasome) | The ubiquitin–proteasome system marks selected proteins for controlled breakdown, while other ubiquitin signals have jobs beyond destruction. |
| [Homologous recombination and HRD](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/genome-instability-and-repair/homologous-recombination-and-hrd) | Homologous recombination repairs damaged DNA using a matching template; homologous recombination deficiency means this repair process is impaired. |
| [AMPK: matching cellular work to energy supply](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/nutrient-sensing-ampk) | AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is an enzyme complex that helps cells adjust energy use and production when their energy state changes. |
| [Cholesterol homeostasis through SCAP–SREBP](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/cholesterol-and-srebp) | SCAP–SREBP regulation helps cells adjust lipid production and uptake in response to their internal sterol supply. |
| [Endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/er-stress-and-unfolded-protein-response) | Endoplasmic reticulum stress occurs when protein-folding demands exceed local capacity, and the unfolded protein response is the signaling program that helps manage that imbalance. |
| [Oxidative phosphorylation: using a proton gradient to make ATP](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/oxidative-phosphorylation) | Oxidative phosphorylation links electron transport to a proton gradient that powers ATP production in mitochondria. |
| [Reactive oxygen species: signals and damage depend on context](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/reactive-oxygen-species) | Reactive oxygen species are chemically distinct oxygen-containing molecules whose effects depend on their identity, location, amount and timing. |
| [Warburg effect: making lactate when oxygen is available](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/warburg-effect) | The Warburg effect is a pattern in which cells convert substantial glucose to lactate even when oxygen is available. |
| [BET bromodomain proteins: reading acetyl marks](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/bet-bromodomain-proteins) | Bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) proteins recognize acetylated protein marks and help regulate gene expression. |
| [MYC: coordinating growth-related gene expression](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/myc) | MYC is a transcriptional regulator that helps coordinate gene programs involved in cell growth, metabolism and proliferation. |
| [Notch signaling: a message between neighboring cells](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/notch-signaling) | Notch signaling commonly begins with contact between a ligand-bearing cell and a receptor-bearing neighbor, leading to release of a fragment that regulates gene expression. |
| [NRF2–KEAP1: regulating a stress-defense program](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/nrf2-keap1) | KEAP1 helps control NRF2 turnover, while stabilized NRF2 can activate genes that protect cells from oxidative and chemical stress. |
| [PI3K–AKT–mTOR: growth signals and resource use](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/pi3k-akt-mtor) | PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling links growth cues and available resources to cellular programs such as protein production, metabolism, and recycling. |
| [RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK: a growth-signal relay](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/mapk-ras-raf) | The RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway relays signals through a molecular switch and protein kinases to change cellular activity and gene expression. |
| [Receptor tyrosine kinases: turning a surface signal into protein phosphorylation](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/receptor-tyrosine-kinases) | Receptor tyrosine kinases are membrane receptors whose intracellular kinase machinery transfers phosphate groups to proteins to transmit a signal. |
| [Wnt–β-catenin: signaling through controlled protein stability](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/wnt-beta-catenin) | Canonical Wnt signaling changes the control of β-catenin breakdown, allowing β-catenin to help regulate gene expression. |
| [Cancer-associated fibroblasts: support cells within tumor stroma](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/tumor-microenvironment/cancer-associated-fibroblasts-and-stroma) | Cancer-associated fibroblasts are fibroblast cells in a tumor's surrounding tissue that can shape its matrix, signals and interactions with other cells. |
| [Extracellular matrix: the material between cells](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/tumor-microenvironment/extracellular-matrix) | The extracellular matrix is a network of molecules outside cells that provides structure and influences how cells attach, move and receive signals. |
| [Hypoxia: oxygen supply falling short of tissue needs](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/tumor-microenvironment/hypoxia) | Hypoxia is a condition in which oxygen availability is insufficient for a tissue's local needs. |
| [Oncogenes and tumor suppressors: two routes to lost control](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/what-cancer-is/oncogene-and-tumor-suppressor) | Oncogenes promote cancer when growth-related functions become overactive, while tumor suppressors normally restrain cancer-promoting processes. |
| [Tumor heterogeneity: one cancer, several populations](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/what-cancer-is/tumor-heterogeneity) | Tumor heterogeneity means cancer cells can differ in their DNA, molecular state or behavior within and between sampled regions. |

## Engineering and manufacturing

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Single-chain variable fragments and single-domain nanobodies](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/binders/scfv-and-vhh-nanobodies) | Single-chain variable fragments and single-domain nanobodies are compact antibody-derived binding formats that use different structures to recognize a molecular target. |
| [Autologous versus allogeneic cell therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/autologous-vs-allogeneic-cell-therapy) | Autologous cell therapy uses the recipient's own cells, while allogeneic cell therapy uses cells from another person. |
| [Chain of identity and custody](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/chain-of-identity-and-custody) | Chain of identity links a product to its source and intended recipient, while chain of custody records who controlled it through each handoff. |
| [Cryopreservation of living cells](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/cryopreservation) | Cryopreservation uses a controlled preservation process and very low temperatures to retain cells for later recovery and use. |
| [Ex vivo cell expansion](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/ex-vivo-cell-expansion) | Ex vivo cell expansion grows a cell population outside the body under controlled conditions, changing its number and potentially its composition and state. |
| [Ex vivo versus in vivo cell engineering](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/ex-vivo-vs-in-vivo-engineering) | Ex vivo and in vivo cell engineering differ in whether cells receive their new instructions outside or inside the body. |
| [Leukapheresis and the collected leukopak](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/leukapheresis) | Leukapheresis collects white blood cells from circulating blood while returning most other blood components to the person. |
| [Vein-to-vein time](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cell-manufacturing/vein-to-vein-time) | Vein-to-vein time is the elapsed interval from collecting a person's cells to administering the manufactured cell product back to that person. |
| [Good manufacturing practice and batch release](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cmc-and-quality/gmp-and-batch-release) | Good manufacturing practice controls how a product is made, while batch release checks whether a particular lot meets its specified requirements. |
| [Manufacturing comparability](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cmc-and-quality/manufacturing-comparability) | Manufacturing comparability asks whether a process change leaves a biological product sufficiently similar for earlier quality, safety and efficacy evidence to remain relevant. |
| [Potency assays](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/cmc-and-quality/potency-assays) | A potency assay measures a product's biological activity relevant to its intended therapeutic action under defined test conditions. |
| [AAV (adeno-associated virus) vector](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/aav-vector) | An AAV vector is an engineered adeno-associated virus particle that delivers a DNA expression cassette, which often persists outside chromosomes but still requires product-specific delivery and safety assessment. |
| [Delivery targeting versus cancer targeting](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/delivery-targeting-vs-cancer-targeting) | Delivery targeting determines which cells receive an intervention, while cancer targeting determines what the resulting therapeutic molecule or immune cell recognizes or acts on. |
| [Lentiviral vector](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/lentiviral-vector) | A lentiviral vector is an engineered viral delivery system that carries genetic instructions into cells. |
| [LNP (lipid nanoparticle)](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/lipid-nanoparticle) | A lipid nanoparticle is a formulated lipid carrier that can protect RNA and help deliver it into cells. |
| [RNA-lipoplex](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/rna-lipoplex) | An RNA-lipoplex is a formulation in which RNA is complexed with lipids, with delivery behavior determined by the specific composition, preparation and route. |
| [Targeted lipid nanoparticles](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/targeted-lnps) | Targeted lipid nanoparticles use formulation or surface features to favor delivery to particular tissues or cells, but enrichment must be distinguished from selective functional expression. |
| [Transposon for cell engineering](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/delivery-vehicles/transposon) | An engineered DNA transposon is a gene-insertion system that uses a transposase enzyme to place a designed cassette into cellular DNA after a separate method delivers the required components. |
| [Codon optimization](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/nucleic-acid-design/codon-optimization) | Codon optimization changes synonymous codons in a coding sequence to influence RNA behavior and protein production while preserving the intended amino-acid sequence. |
| [Double-stranded RNA impurities and purification](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/nucleic-acid-design/dsrna-impurities-and-purification) | Double-stranded RNA impurities are unwanted paired RNA products that can arise during RNA manufacture and alter sensing or translation, so their removal and measurement are product-quality questions. |
| [In vitro transcription](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/nucleic-acid-design/in-vitro-transcription) | In vitro transcription makes RNA from a DNA template using enzymes outside living cells, after which the RNA must be purified and tested for its intended use. |
| [Modified nucleosides in mRNA](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/nucleic-acid-design/modified-nucleosides) | Modified nucleosides are chemically altered RNA building blocks that can change how a message is sensed and translated without changing its intended protein sequence. |
| [mRNA construct anatomy](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/nucleic-acid-design/mrna-construct-anatomy) | An mRNA construct combines a protein-coding message with sequence features that influence its translation, stability and processing. |
| [Multi-epitope constructs](/wiki/education/concepts/engineering-and-manufacturing/nucleic-acid-design/multi-epitope-constructs) | A multi-epitope construct encodes several intended immune targets in one design, but each target still has to survive expression, processing, display and recognition. |

## Evidence and statistics

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Clinical actionability](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/biomarkers/actionability) | Clinical actionability asks whether a finding supports a specific medical decision in the relevant disease and treatment setting. |
| [Prognostic versus predictive biomarkers](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/biomarkers/prognostic-vs-predictive) | A prognostic biomarker is associated with outcome, while a predictive biomarker identifies differences in the effect of a particular treatment. |
| [Absolute risk, relative risk, and number needed to treat](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/effect-measures/absolute-relative-risk-and-nnt) | Absolute risk is the chance of a defined event by a specified time; relative risk compares those chances, and number needed to treat summarizes their difference. |
| [Confidence interval](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/effect-measures/confidence-interval) | A confidence interval shows an estimate's precision using a method with a stated rate of coverage across repeated studies. |
| [Hazard ratio](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/effect-measures/hazard-ratio) | A hazard ratio compares the instantaneous event rates of two groups among people who remain at risk in each group. |
| [Odds ratio](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/effect-measures/odds-ratio) | An odds ratio compares the odds of a defined event in one group with the odds in another group. |
| [P-values and multiple testing](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/effect-measures/p-values-and-multiple-testing) | A p-value describes how incompatible the observed data are with a specified statistical model, while multiple testing creates more opportunities for misleading findings. |
| [Kaplan–Meier curves](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/endpoints/kaplan-meier) | A Kaplan–Meier curve estimates the probability of remaining free of a defined event over time while accounting for incomplete follow-up. |
| [Survival endpoints](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/endpoints/survival-endpoints) | A survival endpoint specifies the starting time and which events count during follow-up. |
| [Lead-time bias](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/reasoning-tools/lead-time-bias) | Lead-time bias occurs when earlier detection lengthens measured survival from diagnosis without changing when the outcome occurs. |
| [Case reports and N-of-1 trials](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/case-reports-and-n-of-1) | A case report describes an individual clinical experience, while an N-of-1 trial prospectively compares interventions within one person using a planned experimental design. |
| [Conference abstracts, preprints and full papers](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/abstract-vs-peer-reviewed) | Publication format tells you how much of a study you can inspect and what review it has received, but does not by itself establish the study's validity. |
| [Controlled perturbation: change one thing and test the response](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/perturbation-and-controls) | A controlled perturbation changes a defined experimental factor and compares the response with conditions that help explain what caused it. |
| [Meta-analysis](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/meta-analysis) | Meta-analysis combines estimates from separate studies using a specified statistical method to answer a defined question. |
| [Observational studies and confounding](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/observational-studies-and-confounding) | An observational study records care or exposures without assigning them, so other differences between groups can distort the apparent effect of a treatment. |
| [Preclinical evidence](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/preclinical-evidence) | Preclinical evidence tests biological effects, mechanisms or safety in experimental systems before or alongside studies of an intervention in people. |
| [Prespecified and exploratory analysis](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/exploratory-vs-prespecified-analysis) | A prespecified analysis is planned before relevant results are examined, while an exploratory analysis investigates additional questions and must retain the context of that search. |
| [Trial phases and randomization](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/study-design/trial-phases-and-randomization) | Trial phases describe the questions a treatment study asks, while randomization assigns participants by chance to make treatment groups more comparable. |
| [Calibration of predicted probabilities](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/tests-and-prediction/calibration) | Calibration describes how closely predicted probabilities agree with observed outcome frequencies in a defined population and time period. |
| [Discrimination: ROC area and concordance](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/tests-and-prediction/auc-and-c-index) | Discrimination describes how well a score orders people with different outcomes; ROC area and concordance summarize defined pairwise ranking comparisons. |
| [False discovery rate](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/tests-and-prediction/false-discovery-rate) | The false discovery rate is the expected fraction of false findings among a procedure’s selected findings, with that fraction defined as zero when none are selected. |
| [Positive predictive value and base rates](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/tests-and-prediction/ppv-and-base-rates) | Positive predictive value is the probability of the defined condition among people with a positive test result. |
| [Thresholds and cutpoints](/wiki/education/concepts/evidence-and-statistics/tests-and-prediction/thresholds-and-cutpoints) | A threshold or cutpoint turns a continuous measurement or score into categories, so its meaning depends on the measurement, intended action and validation context. |

## Immunology

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [ADCC, ADCP and CDC: antibody-guided effector mechanisms](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/adcc-adcp-cdc) | Antibodies can guide immune cells to kill or engulf a coated target, or recruit complement proteins that can damage its membrane. |
| [Antibody isotypes and Fc silencing](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/isotypes-and-fc-silencing) | An antibody's isotype is its heavy-chain class, while Fc (fragment crystallizable) silencing reduces selected interactions that recruit immune effectors. |
| [Antibody structure](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/antibody-structure) | An antibody is an immune protein whose binding regions recognize a molecular feature and whose remaining structure helps determine its biological functions. |
| [B cells and plasma cells](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/b-cells-and-plasma-cells) | B cells participate in antigen recognition and immune responses, while plasma cells are differentiated members of the B-cell lineage specialized for antibody secretion. |
| [Tertiary lymphoid structures](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/tertiary-lymphoid-structures) | Tertiary lymphoid structures are organized immune-cell gatherings that develop in tissues outside native lymphoid organs, with variable architecture and maturity. |
| [Antigen and epitope](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/antigen-and-epitope) | An antigen is a molecule recognized by an immune receptor; an epitope is the particular part or molecular surface that receptor recognizes. |
| [Antigen-processing machinery](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/antigen-processing-machinery) | Antigen-processing machinery generates protein fragments and loads them onto display molecules for T-cell inspection. |
| [B2M (beta-2 microglobulin)](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/b2m) | Beta-2 microglobulin supports the assembly and stable surface expression of classical human leukocyte antigen class-I complexes. |
| [Cross-presentation](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/cross-presentation) | Cross-presentation occurs when a cell displays externally acquired antigen on class-I human leukocyte antigen molecules. |
| [HLA (human leukocyte antigen)](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/hla) | HLA molecules display protein fragments for T-cell recognition. |
| [HLA typing and nomenclature](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/hla-typing-and-nomenclature) | Human leukocyte antigen typing identifies inherited alleles, whose standardized names describe progressively more detailed sequence differences. |
| [Neoantigen](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/neoantigen) | A neoantigen is an immune-recognition target arising from a tumor-specific sequence alteration. |
| [Self-similarity and tolerance](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/self-similarity-and-tolerance) | Immune tolerance restrains responses to self, while a candidate target's resemblance to healthy molecules is one factor in recognition and safety. |
| [Tumor-associated antigen](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antigen-presentation/tumor-associated-antigen) | A tumor-associated antigen is an immune-recognition target associated with tumor expression that may also occur in healthy tissue. |
| [Cytokines and chemokines](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/immune-regulation/cytokine-and-chemokine) | Cytokines are signaling proteins that change cell behavior; chemokines are a cytokine family best known for helping direct cell movement. |
| [Immune checkpoints](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/immune-regulation/immune-checkpoint) | Immune checkpoints are regulatory interactions that adjust immune-cell responses, including inhibitory pathways that help protect healthy tissue. |
| [Peripheral tolerance](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/immune-regulation/peripheral-tolerance) | Peripheral tolerance is the collection of mechanisms that restrain mature immune cells after development, helping prevent harmful responses to self and other tolerated material. |
| [cGAS–STING DNA sensing](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/innate-and-unconventional-cells/cgas-sting) | cGAS–STING is a cellular DNA-sensing pathway that can trigger inflammatory signals, with consequences that depend on the cell and the biological setting. |
| [Dendritic cells](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/innate-and-unconventional-cells/dendritic-cells) | Dendritic cells are immune cells that collect and present antigen and can help start or shape T-cell responses through accompanying signals. |
| [Gamma-delta T cells](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/innate-and-unconventional-cells/gamma-delta-t-cells) | Gamma-delta T cells are a diverse T-cell family whose antigen receptors contain gamma and delta chains and recognize different signals across subsets. |
| [Natural killer cells and missing-self recognition](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/innate-and-unconventional-cells/nk-cell-and-missing-self) | Natural killer cells integrate activating and inhibitory signals, and reduced normal self-display can remove part of the inhibition that restrains them. |
| [Pattern recognition: DAMPs and PAMPs](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/innate-and-unconventional-cells/pattern-recognition-damps-and-pamps) | Pattern-recognition receptors sense molecular features associated with microbes or tissue damage, helping shape innate immune responses without specifying a tumor-specific target. |
| [Costimulation](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/costimulation) | Costimulation is support from additional receptor interactions that helps shape a T cell’s response to antigen recognition. |
| [Epitope spreading](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/epitope-spreading) | Epitope spreading is the extension of a detected immune response to additional epitopes beyond those initially targeted. |
| [Immunodominance](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/immunodominance) | Immunodominance is the unequal distribution of an immune response across the targets available in a particular biological setting. |
| [Regulatory T cells](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/regulatory-t-cells) | Regulatory T cells are T cells that help restrain immune responses and protect tolerance, while sometimes limiting useful antitumor immunity. |
| [T-cell anergy](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/anergy) | T-cell anergy is a state of reduced antigen-driven responsiveness that can follow antigen recognition in a context that does not support productive activation. |
| [T-cell priming and the three-signal model](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/t-cell-priming-three-signals) | T-cell priming is the initial activation of a naive T cell by antigen recognition and a supporting signal context. |
| [T-cell states](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/t-cell-states) | T-cell states describe changing differentiation and functional programs, rather than simply whether a T cell is present. |
| [TCR (T-cell receptor)](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/t-cell-receptor) | A conventional alpha-beta T-cell receptor recognizes a peptide together with the human leukocyte antigen molecule displaying it. |
| [The T-cell repertoire](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/t-cell-repertoire) | The T-cell repertoire is the collection of different T-cell receptors in a population and their abundances at a particular time. |
| [Thymic output and aging](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/t-cells/thymic-output-and-aging) | Thymic output is the release of newly developed T cells from the thymus, which generally declines with age but does not stop at one fixed adult birthday. |
| [Inflammation](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/the-immune-system/inflammation) | Inflammation is a tissue response to infection, injury or disturbed function that mobilizes immune signals, cells and other defenses. |
| [Innate vs adaptive immunity](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/the-immune-system/innate-vs-adaptive-immunity) | Innate immunity uses inherited sensing systems, while adaptive immunity expands cells with diverse antigen-specific receptors. |
| [Interferons](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/the-immune-system/interferons) | Interferons are signaling proteins that change how cells respond to infection, stress and immune activity; their effects depend on the receiving cells and the timing. |
| [Lymph nodes and lymphatics](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/the-immune-system/lymph-nodes-and-lymphatics) | Lymphatic vessels return tissue fluid to the blood and carry immune material to lymph nodes, where organized immune cells can meet. |
| [The immune-cell roster](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/the-immune-system/immune-cell-roster) | Immune cells perform different sensing, presentation, killing, antibody-producing and regulatory jobs that depend on their state and location. |
| [Hot, excluded and cold tumor patterns](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/tumor-immunology/hot-excluded-cold-tumor) | Hot, excluded and cold describe patterns of immune-cell presence and location in sampled tumor tissue, rather than proving tumor recognition or treatment benefit. |
| [Immune escape](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/tumor-immunology/immune-escape) | Immune escape occurs when cancer cells survive by avoiding recognition, access or effective immune attack. |
| [In-situ vaccination](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/tumor-immunology/in-situ-vaccination) | In-situ vaccination is a strategy that uses tumor material within the body as an antigen source while supporting local immune activation to generate a broader antitumor response. |
| [The abscopal effect](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/tumor-immunology/abscopal-effect) | An abscopal effect is a tumor response outside the irradiated area following local radiation, with the cause of that distant response requiring separate assessment. |
| [The cancer-immunity cycle](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/tumor-immunology/cancer-immunity-cycle) | The cancer-immunity cycle is a framework connecting antigen release, T-cell priming, tumor access, recognition and killing in a repeating immune response. |

## Measurement and assays

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Analytical validity, clinical validity and clinical utility](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/computational-analysis/analytical-validity-clinical-validity-and-utility) | Analytical validity asks whether a test measures correctly, clinical validity asks whether its result relates to a clinical condition, and clinical utility asks whether using it helps patients. |
| [Binding prediction](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/computational-analysis/binding-prediction) | Binding prediction estimates a molecular interaction from a model; it does not directly measure peptide display, immune recognition or treatment benefit. |
| [Virtual cell model: a computed biological response](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/computational-analysis/virtual-cell-model) | A virtual cell model uses computation to represent selected cell properties or responses rather than growing a living experimental preparation. |
| [BH3 profiling: testing the mitochondrial death threshold](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/functional-models/bh3-profiling) | BH3 profiling measures how mitochondria respond to defined death-signaling challenges, revealing apoptotic readiness and possible survival-protein dependencies in the sampled cells. |
| [Ex vivo drug screen: response outside the original body](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/functional-models/ex-vivo-drug-screen) | An ex vivo drug screen measures how collected living material responds to drug exposure outside its original organism. |
| [Organoid: a small culture with selected tissue features](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/functional-models/organoid) | An organoid is a three-dimensional cell culture that self-organizes to reproduce selected structural or functional features of a tissue. |
| [Patient-derived xenograft: a tumor growing in another host](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/functional-models/patient-derived-xenograft) | A patient-derived xenograft is a model made by implanting patient tumor material into an animal of another species. |
| [ELISpot (enzyme-linked immunospot)](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/elispot) | ELISpot counts spots formed when sampled cells release a chosen molecule under defined test conditions. |
| [Flow cytometry](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/flow-cytometry) | Flow cytometry measures light signals from individual particles passing through a laser, allowing prepared cells to be counted and grouped by their measured features. |
| [Functional cytotoxicity assays](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/functional-cytotoxicity-assays) | Functional cytotoxicity assays test whether effector cells cause death of specified target cells under controlled laboratory conditions. |
| [Intracellular cytokine staining](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/intracellular-cytokine-staining) | Intracellular cytokine staining measures cytokines retained inside cells during a defined assay, allowing a response to be assigned to stained cell populations. |
| [Peptide–HLA multimer staining](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/peptide-hla-multimer-staining) | Multimer staining labels cells whose receptors bind a specified peptide held by its display molecule under the assay conditions. |
| [Peripheral blood mononuclear cells](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/peripheral-blood-mononuclear-cells) | Peripheral blood mononuclear cells are the circulating white blood cells with a single, unsegmented nucleus, mainly lymphocytes and monocytes, that can be isolated for immune assays. |
| [T-cell receptor clonality metrics](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/tcr-clonality-metrics) | T-cell receptor clonality metrics summarize how unevenly sampled receptor identities are distributed, without identifying what those receptors recognize. |
| [T-cell receptor sequencing](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/immune-monitoring/tcr-sequencing) | T-cell receptor sequencing identifies sampled receptor sequences and their measured abundances, allowing immune populations to be compared across samples. |
| [Cell-free DNA versus circulating tumor DNA](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/cfdna-vs-ctdna) | Cell-free DNA is DNA outside intact cells in a sample; circulating tumor DNA is the portion that comes from tumor cells. |
| [ctDNA (circulating tumor DNA) units and detection limits](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/ctdna-units) | A circulating tumor DNA number is meaningful only with the assay’s measured quantity, units and detection criteria. |
| [Tumor-informed molecular residual disease assays](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/liquid-biopsy/tumor-informed-mrd-assay) | A tumor-informed molecular residual disease assay uses features identified in a tumor sample to look for a qualifying tumor signal in later blood samples. |
| [Immunohistochemistry: protein staining in tissue](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/pathology-readouts/immunohistochemistry) | Immunohistochemistry uses antibodies to visualize selected proteins in tissue while preserving information about the stained cells and their location. |
| [Stromal TIL scoring: what the percentage counts](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/pathology-readouts/stil-scoring) | A stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte score estimates the percentage of eligible tumor-associated stromal area occupied by mononuclear inflammatory cells. |
| [Cell segmentation](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/proteins-and-spatial/cell-segmentation) | Cell segmentation assigns image pixels to estimated cell regions so measurements can be summarized for individual cells. |
| [Imaging mass cytometry](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/proteins-and-spatial/imaging-mass-cytometry) | Imaging mass cytometry maps selected tissue markers by laser-sampling a section stained with metal-tagged antibodies and detecting their metal signals. |
| [Immunopeptidomics](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/proteins-and-spatial/immunopeptidomics) | Immunopeptidomics identifies protein fragments associated with antigen-display molecules in a sampled preparation. |
| [Mass spectrometry proteomics](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/proteins-and-spatial/mass-spectrometry-proteomics) | Mass spectrometry proteomics identifies and measures protein-derived molecules through their mass-to-charge and fragmentation signatures, with conclusions limited by sample preparation, identification and quantification rules. |
| [Multiplex immunofluorescence](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/proteins-and-spatial/multiplex-immunofluorescence) | Multiplex immunofluorescence uses fluorescent stains to map several selected molecular markers in the same tissue section. |
| [Phosphoproteomics](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/proteins-and-spatial/phosphoproteomics) | Phosphoproteomics measures phosphorylated protein-derived molecules and their modification sites to study signaling states, while requiring separate evidence for protein amount, site function and pathway dependence. |
| [Panel, exome and genome sequencing: choose the coverage](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/sequencing-basics/panel-exome-genome) | Panel, exome and genome sequencing examine progressively broader sets of DNA regions, with assay-specific depth and detection limits. |
| [Sequencing depth and coverage](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/sequencing-basics/sequencing-depth-and-coverage) | Sequencing depth counts usable observations at a position, while coverage breadth describes how much of a defined target meets an explicitly stated measurement criterion. |
| [Sequencing reads](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/sequencing-basics/sequencing-reads) | A sequencing read is an instrument-derived sequence of bases from a prepared molecule, with quality information that helps assess how reliably those bases were identified. |
| [Tumor–normal versus tumor-only sequencing](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/sequencing-basics/tumor-normal-vs-tumor-only-sequencing) | Tumor–normal sequencing compares tumor DNA with a suitable normal sample from the same person to help attribute variants, while tumor-only sequencing must infer their origin without that paired comparison. |
| [Variant allele fraction vs cancer cell fraction](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/sequencing-basics/variant-allele-fraction-and-cancer-cell-fraction) | Variant allele fraction measures variant-supporting sequence observations, while cancer cell fraction estimates how many cancer cells carry the change. |
| [Variant calling](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/sequencing-basics/variant-calling) | Variant calling is the computational process of evaluating sequence evidence and error models to identify candidate differences from a reference or comparison sample. |
| [RNA integrity](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/specimens/rna-integrity) | RNA integrity describes the condition and fragmentation of extracted RNA, helping determine which measurement workflows can recover useful information from a specimen. |
| [Batch effects](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/transcriptomics/batch-effects) | A batch effect is a systematic measurement difference associated with preparation, processing or analysis conditions that can be mistaken for a biological difference between samples. |
| [Bulk RNA sequencing: a tissue mixture of messages](/wiki/education/concepts/measurement/transcriptomics/bulk-rna-seq) | Bulk RNA sequencing measures captured RNA from a sample as one combined profile, without directly assigning each message to its cell of origin. |

## Treatment mechanisms

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [ADC (antibody-drug conjugate)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/antibody-based/antibody-drug-conjugate) | An antibody-drug conjugate links a targeting antibody to a drug payload so binding and product-specific processing can deliver chemical damage. |
| [T-cell engager](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/antibody-based/t-cell-engager) | A T-cell engager is a therapeutic molecule that binds a target on another cell and an activation component such as CD3 on T cells to help redirect T-cell attack. |
| [Armoring an immune-cell therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/armoring) | Armoring adds engineered functions to an immune-cell therapy to help it act or survive in a difficult environment, beyond its basic target-recognition receptor. |
| [CAR (chimeric antigen receptor) anatomy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/car-anatomy) | A chimeric antigen receptor connects an external antigen-binding region to internal signaling machinery in an engineered immune cell. |
| [Cell therapy as a living drug](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/cell-therapy-as-a-living-drug) | A cell therapy is a living drug when the administered or newly engineered cells can change their number, location and behavior after treatment. |
| [Chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cells](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/car-nk) | Chimeric antigen receptor natural killer cells combine an engineered recognition receptor with the activating and inhibitory machinery of a natural killer cell. |
| [Engineered T-cell receptor therapy (TCR-T)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/tcr-t) | Engineered T-cell receptor therapy installs a selected receptor in T cells so they can recognize a specified peptide together with its presenting human leukocyte antigen molecule. |
| [Logic-gated chimeric antigen receptors](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/logic-gated-cars) | Logic-gated chimeric antigen receptor systems combine multiple recognition inputs to broaden coverage or restrict when an engineered cell becomes active. |
| [Safety switches in cell therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/safety-switches) | A cell-therapy safety switch is an engineered mechanism intended to reduce specified cell activity or eliminate engineered cells when a control input is applied. |
| [Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/cell-therapies/til-therapy) | Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy collects immune cells from a tumor, expands them outside the body and transfers them back to seek useful antitumor activity. |
| [Alkylating agents](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/alkylating-agents) | Classic alkylating agents are chemotherapy drugs that transfer reactive alkyl groups to cellular molecules, including DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), disrupting functions needed for cell survival. |
| [Anthracyclines](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/anthracyclines) | Anthracyclines are chemotherapy drugs that interact with DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and enzymes such as topoisomerase II, producing cellular injury through several mechanisms. |
| [Capecitabine and fluoropyrimidines](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/capecitabine-and-fluoropyrimidines) | Fluoropyrimidines disrupt nucleotide metabolism and RNA (ribonucleic acid) biology; capecitabine is an oral prodrug that the body converts to the fluoropyrimidine fluorouracil. |
| [Chemotherapy: disrupting cell survival and division](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/chemotherapy) | Cytotoxic chemotherapy uses drugs to disrupt processes such as copying DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) or dividing cells, affecting cancer cells and susceptible normal cells. |
| [Dose-dense scheduling](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/dose-dense-scheduling) | Dose-dense scheduling gives a specified chemotherapy regimen at shorter cycle intervals than its comparison schedule, often increasing the amount delivered per unit of time. |
| [Platinum agents](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/platinum-agents) | Platinum agents are chemotherapy drugs whose reactive platinum complexes attach to DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and can form cross-links that disrupt its use and copying. |
| [Taxanes](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/chemotherapy/taxanes) | Taxanes are chemotherapy drugs that stabilize microtubules, disrupting the flexible protein machinery cells use for division and other functions. |
| [Chemical probes, medicines and target engagement](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/how-drugs-work/chemical-probe-vs-drug-and-target-engagement) | A chemical probe helps test a biological mechanism, while target engagement asks whether a compound reaches and interacts with its intended target under the measured conditions. |
| [Dose and dose intensity](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/how-drugs-work/dose-and-dose-intensity) | Dose is the amount of a drug administered, while dose intensity is the amount delivered per unit of time for a named drug and measurement method. |
| [Pharmacokinetics and half-life](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/how-drugs-work/pharmacokinetics-and-half-life) | Pharmacokinetics describes a product's concentration over time as the body handles it, while half-life describes a specified concentration decline under stated conditions. |
| [Synthetic lethality](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/how-drugs-work/synthetic-lethality) | Synthetic lethality occurs when two impairments together kill a cell even though either impairment alone can be tolerated. |
| [Therapeutic index](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/how-drugs-work/therapeutic-index) | Therapeutic index compares doses producing defined toxic effects with doses producing defined useful effects, helping describe the separation between benefit and harm. |
| [Immune checkpoint inhibitor](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/immunotherapy/immune-checkpoint-inhibitor) | An immune checkpoint inhibitor is a drug that blocks a specific inhibitory immune pathway, which can strengthen responses against cancer and also harm healthy tissue. |
| [Intratumoral immunotherapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/immunotherapy/intratumoral-immunotherapy) | Intratumoral immunotherapy places an immune-directed treatment inside a tumor, while local effects, immune recognition and effects elsewhere remain separate questions. |
| [Oncolytic viruses](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/immunotherapy/oncolytic-virus) | An oncolytic virus is a virus studied or used to infect and damage cancer cells, sometimes also helping the immune system respond to tumor material. |
| [Ablation](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/ablation) | Tumor ablation destroys tissue at a selected site using a defined physical or chemical method, with coverage and safety assessed for the target and nearby structures. |
| [Hypofractionation](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/hypofractionation) | Hypofractionation gives radiation in larger doses per treatment than a conventional comparison schedule, usually reducing the number of treatments in a course. |
| [Radiation therapy basics](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/radiation-therapy-basics) | Radiation therapy uses ionizing radiation to damage cancer cells, with the treated volume and schedule chosen for a defined clinical goal. |
| [Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/local-therapies/sbrt) | Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) delivers a high dose per fraction to a defined target outside the brain using precise positioning, imaging and a small number of treatments. |
| [Cytokine release syndrome (CRS)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/cytokine-release-syndrome) | Cytokine release syndrome is a systemic inflammatory response that can follow immune activation and cause fever, low blood pressure or low oxygen. |
| [Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/icans) | ICANS is a neurologic syndrome associated with immune-effector-cell therapy that is assessed through clinical changes in brain function. |
| [Immune-related adverse event (irAE)](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/immune-related-adverse-event) | An immune-related adverse event is harm to healthy tissue attributed to treatment-related immune activity. |
| [Insertional mutagenesis](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/insertional-mutagenesis) | Insertional mutagenesis occurs when added genetic material changes a cell by inserting into its genome and disrupting a gene or its regulation. |
| [Lymphodepletion before cell therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/lymphodepletion) | Lymphodepletion reduces existing lymphocytes before some cell therapies to change the immune environment in which the therapeutic cells must work. |
| [On-target, off-tumor toxicity](/wiki/education/concepts/therapeutics/toxicities-and-supportive-care/on-target-off-tumor-toxicity) | On-target, off-tumor toxicity occurs when a treatment recognizes its intended target on healthy tissue and harms that tissue. |
