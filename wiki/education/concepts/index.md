# Education concepts

Use a concept to answer one question; use a [guide](/wiki/education/index) to learn toward a goal. These summaries and the glossary definitions are generated from the concept pages. Domain and learner review remain pending where stated on each page.

## Access and regulation

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Companion diagnostics](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/companion-diagnostics) | A companion diagnostic supplies information essential to the safe and effective use of a corresponding treatment in its specified context. |
| [Drug approval pathways and expedited designations](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/fda-approval-types) | Drug approval authorizes a specified use, while expedited designations change parts of development or review without themselves authorizing marketing. |
| [Drug labeling and off-label use](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/drug-label-and-off-label-use) | Off-label use means a clinician uses an approved drug outside its approved labeling, while the evidence for that particular use must be assessed separately. |
| [Research-use, laboratory certification and test authorization](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/approval/ruo-ldt-clia-and-fda-tiers) | Research-use labeling, laboratory certification and product authorization answer different questions about a test and do not form one universal ladder of clinical usefulness. |
| [Clinical trials versus expanded access](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/clinical-trial-vs-expanded-access) | A clinical trial studies a treatment under a research protocol, while expanded access uses an investigational product for treatment outside a trial under applicable safeguards. |
| [Finding and checking a clinical trial](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/finding-a-trial) | Finding a trial means identifying a study and then confirming its protocol, relevant cohort, site availability and formal eligibility with the study team. |
| [IND investigation versus BLA marketing review](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/ind-vs-bla) | An investigational application supports defined human use during research or access, while a marketing application asks for authorization to market a product for a specified use. |
| [Investigator-initiated trials](/wiki/education/concepts/access-and-regulation/trials-and-access/investigator-initiated-trials) | An investigator-initiated trial starts with a researcher's study proposal, while sponsor responsibilities, product oversight and participant protections still depend on the actual protocol and regulatory arrangement. |

## Biology basics

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Cell division and why chemotherapy can disrupt it](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/cell-division-and-why-chemo-hits-dividing-cells) | Cell division produces daughter cells by copying and separating cellular material; chemotherapy can disrupt these tasks without affecting only dividing cells. |
| [Cell lineage and differentiation](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/cell-lineage-and-differentiation) | Cell lineage describes developmental ancestry, while differentiation is the process through which cells acquire specialized features and functions. |
| [Epithelial–mesenchymal transition: changing cell programs](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/epithelial-mesenchymal-transition) | Epithelial–mesenchymal transition is a context-dependent process in which epithelial cells lose some epithelial features and acquire some mesenchymal features, often through intermediate states. |
| [Epithelium and stroma: reading tissue compartments](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/epithelium-and-stroma) | Epithelium forms tissue coverings, linings and glands; stroma is the supporting tissue around and between those structures, containing cells and extracellular material. |
| [Organelles: the cell's working compartments](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/organelles-overview) | Organelles are structures inside a cell that organize particular jobs, including storing genetic information, processing cargo, recycling material and supporting metabolism. |
| [The cell and its membrane](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/cell-and-membrane) | A cell is a living unit enclosed by a membrane that separates its contents from its surroundings and controls exchange. |
| [The cell cycle: preparing, copying and dividing](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/cells/cell-cycle) | The cell cycle is a regulated sequence in which a cell prepares, copies its genetic material and divides; some cells remain outside that sequence. |
| [Alleles and zygosity: versions are different from copies](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/allele-and-zygosity) | An allele is a version of DNA at a particular location; zygosity describes whether the versions present at that location are the same or different. |
| [Central dogma: from DNA to RNA to protein](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/central-dogma) | For a protein-coding gene, cells transcribe DNA into RNA and translate the RNA into protein. |
| [Epigenetics and DNA methylation](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/epigenetics-and-methylation) | Epigenetic regulation changes how cells use DNA without rewriting its base sequence; DNA methylation is one chemical mark that can influence that regulation. |
| [Germline vs somatic: where a variant belongs](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/germline-vs-somatic) | Germline variants can be passed through eggs or sperm, while somatic variants arise in other cell lineages. |
| [Mutation types: what changed in the DNA?](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/mutation-types) | A DNA variant describes a difference in sequence or copy structure; its biological effect is a separate question. |
| [Nonsense-mediated decay: a context-dependent RNA fate](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/nonsense-mediated-decay) | Nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) is an RNA-surveillance process that can reduce messages with abnormal translation termination; its effect depends on the transcript and cellular context. |
| [RNA splicing](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/splicing) | RNA splicing removes introns and joins retained exons into a processed message; different choices can produce different transcripts from the same gene. |
| [V(D)J recombination: assembling immune receptors](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/genetics/vdj-recombination) | V(D)J recombination joins selected gene segments to create the antigen-recognizing parts of B-cell and T-cell receptors. |
| [DNA: storing, copying and reading sequence](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/molecules/dna) | Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a molecule whose base sequence stores genetic information that cells can copy and use to make RNA. |
| [Enzymes and binding: interaction is not reaction](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/molecules/enzymes-and-binding) | Binding is an interaction between molecular partners; an enzyme catalyzes a chemical reaction, so binding strength and reaction rate describe different properties. |
| [Gene: a region that produces a functional message](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/molecules/gene) | A gene is a region of DNA used to produce a functional RNA, which may itself do a job or provide instructions for making a protein. |
| [Protein structure and domains](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/molecules/protein-structure-and-domains) | Protein structure is the three-dimensional arrangement of an amino-acid chain; domains are regions that form recognizable structural units within a protein. |
| [Proteins and peptides: amino-acid molecules](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/molecules/protein-and-peptide) | Proteins and peptides are molecules built from amino-acid chains; their sequence, processing and physical form help determine what they can do. |
| [RNA and messenger RNA: messages with different jobs](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/molecules/rna-and-mrna) | Ribonucleic acid (RNA) is a family of molecules with many cellular jobs; messenger RNA (mRNA) is the kind that supplies a readable template for making a protein. |
| [Agonists and antagonists: binding versus action](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/agonist-and-antagonist) | An agonist promotes a receptor's response, an antagonist opposes agonist action, and an inverse agonist reduces a receptor's activity that exists without an agonist. |
| [Feedback loops: when a signal changes its own circuit](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/feedback-loops) | A feedback loop returns an effect to an earlier part of a circuit, where it can restrain or reinforce the activity that helped produce it. |
| [Ligands and receptors](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/ligand-and-receptor) | A ligand is a molecule that binds a particular partner; a receptor is a protein that senses a signal and can change a cell’s behavior. |
| [Phosphorylation: a modification with different meanings](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/phosphorylation) | Protein phosphorylation attaches a phosphate group at a particular site, where it can increase activity, reduce activity or change a protein's interactions and location. |
| [Receptor types: where a signal is received](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/receptor-types) | Receptor types describe where a signal is received and how the receiving protein connects that signal to a cellular response. |
| [Signal transduction and kinases: following the relay](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/signal-transduction-and-kinases) | Signal transduction converts an input into a cellular response through interacting molecules, with kinases often relaying information by transferring phosphate groups to specific substrates. |

## Breast cancer and pathology

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| [Breast anatomy: ducts, lobules and tissue compartments](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/breast-anatomy) | Breast tissue contains branching ducts connected to milk-producing lobules, with supporting stroma, blood vessels and lymphatics around them. |
| [Breast lymphatic drainage and the axilla](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/lymphatic-drainage-and-axilla) | Breast lymphatic drainage carries tissue fluid through lymphatic vessels to regional nodes, commonly in the axilla, with additional routes including the internal mammary chain. |
| [Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/dcis) | Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) describes abnormal breast cells confined to a milk duct, without invasion into the surrounding breast tissue. |
| [Internal mammary nodes: regional nodes beside the breastbone](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/internal-mammary-nodes) | Internal mammary lymph nodes lie beside the breastbone along the internal thoracic vessels and are a regional nodal group for the breast on the same side. |
| [Invasive ductal carcinoma and the name “no special type”](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/invasive-ductal-carcinoma) | Invasive ductal carcinoma usually refers to invasive breast carcinoma of no special type, a histologic category in which malignant epithelial cells invade surrounding tissue without the defining pattern of a special histologic type. |
| [Myoepithelial cells and the breast basement membrane](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/anatomy-and-histology/myoepithelial-cells-and-basement-membrane) | Myoepithelial cells form an outer epithelial layer around typical normal breast ducts and acini, while the basement membrane is a separate layer of extracellular material between the epithelial structure and surrounding stroma. |
| [AJCC prognostic staging: extent, biology, and the right context](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/ajcc-prognostic-staging) | AJCC breast prognostic staging combines anatomical disease extent with histologic grade and receptor results under edition-specific rules for clinical or surgery-first pathological staging. |
| [Histologic grade](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/histologic-grade) | Histologic grade describes a breast cancer's microscopic features; the Nottingham system combines tubule formation, nuclear appearance and mitotic activity into grades 1, 2 or 3. |
| [Ki-67 proliferation index](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/ki-67) | Ki-67 is a protein associated with the active cell cycle; its proliferation index is the percentage of evaluated tumor-cell nuclei that stain positive under a specified method. |
| [N categories and nodal burden: location, size, count, and timing](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/n-categories-and-nodal-burden) | Breast-cancer N categories describe regional lymph-node involvement using clinical or pathological rules, while nodal burden includes deposit size, node count, location, and how the nodes were assessed. |
| [TNM staging](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/grade-and-stage/tnm-staging) | TNM staging describes the primary tumor, regional lymph nodes and distant metastasis using a cancer-specific classification. |
| [BRCAness: a resemblance that needs evidence](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/hereditary-risk/brcaness) | BRCAness is a research term for features resembling BRCA1- or BRCA2-deficient cancers in tumors without a known BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation. |
| [Germline testing: reading an inherited-risk result](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/hereditary-risk/germline-testing) | Germline testing looks for constitutional genetic variants that may explain inherited susceptibility to cancer. |
| [Hereditary breast cancer genes: BRCA1, BRCA2 and PALB2](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/hereditary-risk/hereditary-breast-cancer-genes) | Hereditary breast cancer genes are genes in which certain inherited harmful variants increase the chance of developing breast cancer. |
| [Brain metastasis risk: keep the population and clock attached](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/natural-history/brain-metastasis-risk) | Brain metastasis risk is the chance of cancer spread to the brain within a specified population, starting point and time window. |
| [Oligometastatic disease: limited visible spread](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/natural-history/oligometastatic-disease) | Oligometastatic disease describes a limited burden of detected distant metastases under a specified clinical or study definition. |
| [TNBC recurrence: timing and sites](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/natural-history/tnbc-recurrence-timing-and-sites) | Recurrence patterns describe when and where triple-negative breast cancer returns in a defined group after treatment. |
| [Estrogen receptor (ER)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/estrogen-receptor) | The estrogen receptor (ER) is a protein inside cells that responds to estrogen; in breast cancer, ER status describes tumor-cell receptor staining under validated testing rules. |
| [HER2: receptor biology and test status](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/her2) | Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is a cell-surface signaling protein; clinical HER2 status describes overexpression or gene amplification under breast-cancer testing rules. |
| [Intrinsic breast-cancer subtype](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/intrinsic-subtype) | An intrinsic breast-cancer subtype is a category assigned by comparing a tumor sample's gene-expression pattern with reference patterns. |
| [Progesterone receptor (PR)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/progesterone-receptor) | The progesterone receptor (PR) is a protein inside cells that responds to progesterone; breast-cancer PR status reports receptor staining in tumor-cell nuclei. |
| [SOX10 and basal markers: tissue clues with limits](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/sox10-and-basal-markers) | SOX10 and basal markers are tissue protein stains that can support a diagnostic interpretation, but cannot alone establish breast origin, a gene-expression subtype, or treatment benefit. |
| [TNBC molecular subtypes: four research profiles, with tissue context](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/tnbc-molecular-subtypes) | TNBC molecular subtypes are research categories assigned from gene-expression patterns under a specified classifier, including the four Lehmann TNBCtype-4 groups. |
| [Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/receptors-and-subtypes/tnbc) | Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is breast cancer classified as negative for estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) by validated tissue tests. |
| [Extranodal extension: beyond a lymph-node capsule](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/extranodal-extension) | Extranodal extension means a cancer deposit in a lymph node extends through its capsule into adjacent tissue; it does not by itself establish distant metastasis. |
| [Lumpectomy versus mastectomy: the extent of breast surgery](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/lumpectomy-vs-mastectomy) | Lumpectomy removes a breast lesion and surrounding tissue while retaining the rest of the breast; mastectomy removes breast tissue more extensively, with different options for preserving skin or the nipple. |
| [Pathologic complete response (pCR)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/pcr) | Pathologic complete response means no residual invasive cancer in the breast or sampled regional nodes after neoadjuvant treatment. |
| [Residual cancer burden (RCB)](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/residual-cancer-burden) | Residual cancer burden combines breast tumor-bed measurements and regional-node findings into a score after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. |
| [Sentinel-node biopsy and axillary dissection: what was sampled?](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/sentinel-node-and-axillary-dissection) | Sentinel-node biopsy removes selected nodes identified by lymphatic mapping, while axillary lymph-node dissection removes a broader group of nodes and surrounding tissue from the armpit region. |
| [Surgical margins](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/surgical-margins) | A surgical margin is the edge of removed tissue, examined to determine whether cancer reaches that edge. |
| [Treatment effect in pathology: response changes and residual disease](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/surgery-and-pathology-results/treatment-effect-in-pathology) | Treatment effect in pathology describes tissue changes consistent with prior therapy, assessed separately from whether residual viable cancer is identified and how much remains in the examined specimen. |
| [Curative intent](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/curative-intent) | Curative intent means that a treatment plan aims to eliminate cancer durably, without guaranteeing that the cancer will never return. |
| [Line of therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/line-of-therapy) | A line of therapy is a distinct treatment strategy in a defined disease setting, counted using the clinical record or a study’s stated rules. |
| [Neoadjuvant versus adjuvant therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/neoadjuvant-vs-adjuvant) | Neoadjuvant and adjuvant describe treatment's timing around the main treatment, usually surgery: neoadjuvant comes before it, and adjuvant comes after it to lower recurrence risk. |
| [Response-adapted therapy](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/response-adapted-therapy) | Response-adapted therapy uses an assessed treatment response to guide a predefined next treatment step within a specified clinical strategy. |
| [The KEYNOTE-522 regimen](/wiki/education/concepts/breast-cancer/treatment-setting/the-keynote-522-regimen) | The KEYNOTE-522 regimen combines pembrolizumab with two chemotherapy blocks before surgery, then pembrolizumab alone afterward, as a studied strategy for high-risk early triple-negative breast cancer. |

## Cancer biology

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|

| [Autophagy: recycling, flow, and dependence](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/autophagy) | Autophagy delivers cellular material to lysosomes for breakdown and reuse; measuring how much material is present does not establish how fast it moves. |
| [Cellular senescence](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/senescence) | Cellular senescence is a state in which living cells develop a durable arrest of division together with other changes that depend on the cell and its context. |

| [Immunogenic cell death](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/immunogenic-cell-death) | Immunogenic cell death is regulated cell death that can initiate an antigen-specific adaptive immune response in a compatible, immune-competent host. |
| [Necrosis](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/necrosis) | Necrosis is a pattern of cell and tissue breakdown involving loss of cell-membrane integrity, which can result from severe injury or regulated death mechanisms. |
| [Ubiquitin and the proteasome](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/cell-death-and-survival/ubiquitin-and-proteasome) | The ubiquitin–proteasome system marks selected proteins for controlled breakdown, while other ubiquitin signals have jobs beyond destruction. |
| [BRCA1 and BRCA2](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/genome-instability-and-repair/brca1-and-brca2) | BRCA1 and BRCA2 are genes whose protein products help preserve genome stability through homologous recombination and other functions associated with DNA repair. |
| [DNA damage and repair: an overview](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/genome-instability-and-repair/dna-damage-and-repair-overview) | DNA repair is a collection of processes that recognize and correct damage to genetic material, with different routes for different kinds of damage. |
| [Genomic scars](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/genome-instability-and-repair/genomic-scars) | Genomic scars are persistent patterns of chromosome imbalance or rearrangement that record aspects of a tumor’s past evolution, rather than directly measuring its current repair activity. |

| [AMPK: matching cellular work to energy supply](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/nutrient-sensing-ampk) | AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is an enzyme complex that helps cells adjust energy use and production when their energy state changes. |
| [Cholesterol homeostasis through SCAP–SREBP](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/cholesterol-and-srebp) | SCAP–SREBP regulation helps cells adjust lipid production and uptake in response to their internal sterol supply. |
| [Endoplasmic reticulum stress and the unfolded protein response](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/er-stress-and-unfolded-protein-response) | Endoplasmic reticulum stress occurs when protein-folding demands exceed local capacity, and the unfolded protein response is the signaling program that helps manage that imbalance. |
| [Oxidative phosphorylation: using a proton gradient to make ATP](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/oxidative-phosphorylation) | Oxidative phosphorylation links electron transport to a proton gradient that powers ATP production in mitochondria. |
| [Reactive oxygen species: signals and damage depend on context](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/reactive-oxygen-species) | Reactive oxygen species are chemically distinct oxygen-containing molecules whose effects depend on their identity, location, amount and timing. |
| [Warburg effect: making lactate when oxygen is available](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/metabolism-and-stress/warburg-effect) | The Warburg effect is a pattern in which cells convert substantial glucose to lactate even when oxygen is available. |
| [Androgen receptor signaling: a hormone-responsive transcription program](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/androgen-receptor-signaling) | Androgen receptor signaling links androgen binding to changes in gene transcription, with effects determined by the receptor, its partners, and the cell context. |
| [BET bromodomain proteins: reading acetyl marks](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/bet-bromodomain-proteins) | Bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) proteins recognize acetylated protein marks and help regulate gene expression. |
| [MYC: coordinating growth-related gene expression](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/myc) | MYC is a transcriptional regulator that helps coordinate gene programs involved in cell growth, metabolism and proliferation. |
| [Notch signaling: a message between neighboring cells](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/notch-signaling) | Notch signaling commonly begins with contact between a ligand-bearing cell and a receptor-bearing neighbor, leading to release of a fragment that regulates gene expression. |
| [NRF2–KEAP1: regulating a stress-defense program](/wiki/education/concepts/cancer-biology/signaling-pathways/nrf2-keap1) | KEAP1 helps control NRF2 turnover, while stabilized NRF2 can activate genes that protect cells from oxidative and chemical stress. |
