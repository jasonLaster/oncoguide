# Ask what happens after binding

**A molecule finding its partner is the start of a mechanism. What happens after binding is a separate question.**

If a diagram has ever left you wondering why one arrow means “grow” and another means “stop,” you are in the right place. This lesson gives you a small set of questions to use when reading any signaling or antibody diagram.

**Before you start:** [Ligands and receptors](/wiki/education/concepts/biology-basics/signaling/ligand-and-receptor) explains the message and receiver. [Antibody structure](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/antibodies-and-b-cells/antibody-structure) separates recognition from other functions.

## Where this step sits

This is lesson 1 of [the immune-system guide](/wiki/education/guides/how-the-immune-system-fights-cancer/index). We start with molecular interactions, then follow how cells collect information, respond and reach a tumor.

**Binding → signaling context → cell behavior → measured effect.**

## The address and the action are different

Picture two parcels arriving at the same address. One contains a request; the other contains a tool. Sharing an address does not make them do the same job. Molecular treatments work in a similar way, although cells are much less tidy than a postal system.

A ligand can activate a receptor. A blocking antibody can interfere with an interaction. A targeting molecule can carry a payload to an accessible protein. Those are three different actions. The name of the bound protein does not tell you which action occurred.

The first useful reading question is therefore, “What binds what?” The next is, “What changes as a result?” Finish with, “Which experiment actually measured that change?” A picture of two molecules touching answers only the first question.

## Follow the effect past the membrane

Cells integrate many signals. A receptor can recruit internal signaling proteins, alter gene activity or change movement. A blocking molecule can reduce those events. The result depends on the receiver, its partners and the cell’s existing state. It is not determined by binding strength alone.

[Immune checkpoints](/wiki/education/concepts/immunology/immune-regulation/immune-checkpoint) provide a useful example. Programmed cell death protein 1, or PD-1, is an inhibitory receptor. Blocking a relevant interaction can reduce inhibition of a T-cell response. The drug does not supply a new cancer target to every T cell. Recognition and access still matter.

Some binding targets serve mainly as addresses. A drug can attach to a surface protein and deliver a payload even when that protein is not the cancer’s main growth signal. Conversely, a growth-related receptor may be present without the tumor depending on it. Presence, dependency and drug effect are three separate claims.

## A worked example: one address, two packages

Consider a fictional surface protein called A and two laboratory molecules:

| Experiment | Observation | Supported claim | Question still open |
|---|---|---|---|
| A plain antibody binds A | Binding is detected | The antibody can reach and bind A under these conditions | Does it alter signaling or recruit a useful effector? |
| The same binder carries a payload | Cancer cells are damaged in the assay | The finished construct has activity in this model | Which cells were exposed, and what happens in healthy tissue? |
| A blocker reduces an internal signal | A pathway readout falls | The interaction affects that measured pathway | Does the tumor depend on it enough for useful growth control? |

These are invented experiments, not drug results. The exercise shows why a targeting assay and an activity assay deserve separate rows in a report.

## What can go wrong at this step

The common shortcut is to replace a chain of evidence with a target name. “Protein A is present” becomes “the drug will work.” Several links may be missing: enough accessible target, a working payload or signaling effect, tumor dependence, and a usable safety window.

Diagrams can also show the wrong partner. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, or HER2, has no known direct ligand; it can signal through partnerships with other receptors in its family. A generic growth factor drawn binding directly to HER2 teaches the wrong interaction. The [primary structural study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34759323/) distinguishes ligand binding from receptor partnership.

## Try it

A laboratory report says a new antibody binds a cancer-cell protein strongly. Can you conclude that it switches off cancer growth?

**Answer:** No. Ask whether it blocks a relevant signal, recruits an effector or delivers a payload, and which downstream effect was measured. Strong binding alone does not establish any of those effects.

## Explain it back

“Binding tells me ___; the next experiment should test ___.”

One possible answer: “which interaction is possible; what changes in the cell after that interaction.”

## Takeaway

**Name the binding partner, the action after binding and the measurement that supports the action.**
